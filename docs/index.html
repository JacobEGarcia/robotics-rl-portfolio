Closing the reality gap

Eighteen projects on what it takes to measure a robot-learning claim honestly, from the scaling laws of imitation to the fluid budget of a body that runs on water.

Chapter One

Scaling laws, at tabletop scale

Robot foundation models are reported to follow power laws: more pretraining data, predictably less error, and small models that stop absorbing while large ones keep going. Which of those shapes appear in a manipulation universe small enough to sweep end-to-end on one CPU — and which do not?

Generalist's GEN-0 report measured its scaling claims on 270,000 hours of real manipulation data and models past ten billion parameters. Those numbers are out of reach here. The experimental design is not: a data engine, a pretraining grid over model size and data scale, validation error on a task family the model never sees, post-training on that family with small demonstration budgets, and closed-loop evaluation with the statistics done properly. Every one of those pieces can be built honestly at small scale. This chapter builds them, and reports what they showed — including the two phenomena they did not.

The universe is a Franka Panda on a tabletop and six task families — reach, push, lift, place, topple, and stack — with every episode procedurally resampled: object sizes, masses, frictions, poses, and goals. The policy never sees the same tabletop twice. Five families form the pretraining corpus. Stack is withheld entirely. Its component skills are all in the corpus — place is already reach, grasp, carry and release into a zone — but the target is a second cube, the tolerance is tighter and the release is raised. The skills transfer; the task does not come for free, as the zero-shot numbers below show.

Twenty-eight simulator processes collect the corpus at 129 times real time: 33.8 hours of demonstrated experience, 74,393 episodes, 2.4 million control steps, in a quarter of an hour. It is stored so that cumulative hours per family stay balanced at every prefix. That detail is the whole data-scaling axis: the 1-hour dataset is literally the first hour of the 32-hour dataset, and seven nested prefixes are compared rather than seven independently sampled datasets whose sampling luck would be confounded with scale.

The grid

Five policy sizes from 11 thousand to 3.3 million parameters, roughly log-spaced, times seven data scales from half an hour to thirty-two hours, times two seeds. Seventy runs, one recipe: AdamW, warmup and cosine decay, thirty epoch-equivalents capped at 150k steps, the best checkpoint by held-in validation loss. The cap binds only at thirty-two hours, where it allows 17.6 epochs. Nothing is tuned per cell, because a scaling study's conclusions should survive a reader asking whether the small runs were simply tuned harder — and an earlier draft of this grid failed exactly that test: a minimum-step floor had quietly given the half-hour cells sixty epochs. Those cells were rerun.

Policies are MLPs predicting eight-step action chunks from a 69-dimensional state. Both are deliberate. The observation is a single state vector, not a token sequence, so attention would have nothing to attend over; and the phenomena under study — loss curves, capacity saturation, transfer — are properties of capacity against data, for which MLP width is the cleanest possible axis. Seventy training runs on a CPU is the budget that makes a sweep possible at all; a perception stack would spend it answering a different question.

What did not appear

GEN-0 describes a threshold below which models ossify — stop converting data into loss — while larger ones keep absorbing. This grid does not show it. Over the doubling from eight to sixteen hours, where every cell trains the identical recipe, all five sizes improved by almost the same fraction: between 8.7% and 11.9%. The small models' gains do shrink in the final doubling — 2.4% for the two smallest against 12% for the two largest — but that is the one doubling where the step cap binds, and the smallest models pick their best checkpoint within the last few thousand steps of the budget, still improving when cut off. Capacity saturation and training budget are confounded at exactly the point the claim would rest on, so the claim is not made. A grid this size may simply be below the threshold; it may also be that a 69-dimensional state does not contain enough to saturate eleven thousand parameters in thirty-two hours. Either way, the honest report is that the phenomenon was looked for and not found.

What the withheld family sees

Every thousand steps, each model is also scored on demonstrations of stack — a family it never trains on. That is the Figure-1 measurement of the GEN-0 report, at this scale: zero-shot prediction error on a withheld downstream task, as a function of pretraining. The number reported is the error at eight epochs of training — a rule fixed without looking at the withheld family. An earlier draft reported the minimum over training, which is an oracle choice on the test family, and one whose bias grows with data scale because longer runs are evaluated more often. It was replaced.

Does pretraining buy downstream success?

The thesis a scaling law exists to support is not about validation loss. It is that pretraining scale buys downstream performance once a model is post-trained on a little task-specific data. So each pretrained checkpoint is post-trained on ten, thirty, or one hundred stack demonstrations with one fixed recipe, and compared against the identical recipe from scratch. The comparison is paired: every arm, both seeds, faces the same hundred sampled stack instances, and what is reported is the seed-averaged per-instance difference with a bootstrap interval over those hundred instances, alongside an exact McNemar test on the discordant pairs. Twenty-one comparisons are made per model size, so a Bonferroni threshold is reported too.

The largest model tells a different story, and it is the one that makes the first credible. From scratch, its 3.3 million parameters fit a hundred demonstrations well enough for 25% success on their own, and pretraining does not significantly change that at any scale: the paired differences run from −6 to +8 points and every interval spans zero. Where the same model is starved — ten or thirty demonstrations — pretraining is worth two to fourteen and five to twenty-four points respectively, and twelve of its twenty-one comparisons are significant. Pretraining pays where data is scarce relative to capacity. An earlier draft of this paragraph reported that half an hour of pretraining made the large model significantly worse; that cell had been trained for sixty epochs by the floor bug above, and after the rerun the effect is −6 points with an interval spanning zero. It is reported here as what it is: not significant.

The validation set that moved

A metric that changes with the axis it is plotted against is not a metric

Each cell of the grid validated, during training, on the held-out episodes of its own data prefix — so every point on the hours axis was scored on a different set of episodes. On the committed runs, that own-prefix error still rises from sixteen to thirty-two hours for three of the five sizes; the thirty-two-hour episodes happen to be harder.

Because episodes are assigned to train or validation by a hash of their seed, a validation episode is held out from every cell regardless of scale. The union of them is a legitimate common test set for the whole grid. Re-scoring all seventy checkpoints on it — no retraining, metrics as functions of what was saved — is what Plate 3 shows, and on it every curve is monotone.

Review before compute

Three of the bugs below were found before the first training run

An adversarial review of the code ran before the sweep did, on the grounds that a silent bug found after seventy training runs costs seventy training runs. It found that resizing an object at reset left its collision bounding radius stale — tall pillars were settling embedded in the table with their contact flickering on one tick in five — that the sampled friction coefficients were being masked by MuJoCo's max-combination rule, and that parked objects carried stale sizes from the previous episode. All three were fixed, verified, and the corpus regenerated, before any number existed to be attached to. A second review of the finished write-up found the step floor and the oracle metric above, and softened more claims than this paragraph has room to list; the chapter is the version that survived it.

What this is and is not

This is a faithful miniature of a scaling-law study: nested data prefixes, a common validation set, a genuinely withheld task family, paired closed-loop comparison with corrected significance, Wilson intervals on success rates, and every number traceable to a committed run log. It is not a claim about what a ten-billion-parameter model does. The observations are state-based rather than pixels, the policies are MLPs, the demonstrators are scripted, two seeds are not many, and the largest model here would be a rounding error in a foundation model's parameter count. What appeared: near-straight loss curves with similar exponents across a 290× range of model size, specialisation away from an unseen task within the first fifteen passes over the data, and pretraining that buys downstream success where demonstrations are scarce relative to capacity. What did not: an ossification threshold, or any return to capacity past 160k parameters between two and sixteen hours of data. Both halves of that list are the result.

Chapter Two

A simulator that has never been wrong is not a simulator

Fitting MuJoCo to 632 seconds of logged motion from a physical Franka Panda, and reporting the number that counts: error on episodes the optimiser never saw.

The DROID dataset records two signals that most robot logs conflate. One is what the controller was commanded to do. The other is where the arm actually went. They are never the same. Real joints carry friction, rotor inertia, finite controller stiffness, and a communication delay, and the difference between command and outcome is the physical fingerprint of all of it.

That difference is the thing a simulator has to reproduce before it is worth training a policy in. So the protocol is blunt: take the real commanded positions, replay them open-loop through a simulated Panda, and see how far it drifts from the real arm. The simulator is initialised from the robot's first measured state and then never shown ground truth again. It cannot be dragged along by the answer.

Menagerie's Panda — a well-maintained, widely used model — starts with an open-loop error of 0.0431 rad against the real arm. After fitting five parameters with CMA-ES, that falls to 0.0187 rad on held-out episodes.

The split matters more than the number. Parameters were optimised on 24 episodes and scored on 8 the optimiser never touched, split by episode rather than by frame, because frames inside one trajectory are heavily correlated and a frame-level split leaks. Fitting and reporting on the same data is overfitting a simulator; it always looks good and it means nothing.

What the fit says about the model

The identified parameters are more interesting than the error figure. Menagerie ships the Panda with zero dry friction and zero latency. The fit disagrees on both.

Plates 13 & 14 Convergence over 45 CMA-ES generations, and the improvement broken out per joint — between 46% and 74%, largest at the wrist where the light distal links make friction and delay dominate.

The dataset that had no answer in it

A perfect fit is usually evidence of a broken measurement

The first attempt used a different Franka dataset. Its action column turned out to be exactly state[t+1] − state[t] — correlation 1.0000 on every joint, standard deviations identical to the last digit. The "commands" were relabelled state differences.

That dataset is self-consistent by construction. It contains no gap, because nothing in it was ever measured independently. Fitting a simulator to it would have produced a beautiful, meaningless result — and nothing about the process would have flagged it.

The check now runs before the optimiser does. If commanded and measured are not independent signals, the code refuses to fit rather than publishing a flattering plot.

Chapter Three

Tendons do not behave like joints

A two-jointed finger pulled by an opposing pair of artificial muscles, and the two very different reasons it fails to go where you tell it.

Geared joints are stiff, reversible, and boring. Tendon-driven ones — the arrangement used by musculoskeletal androids, and by your hand — are none of those things. A tendon can pull but never push, so every joint needs an antagonist. It routes over curved surfaces, so its leverage changes with angle. And it drags, so the joint arrives somewhere different depending on which direction it came from.

Driving the flexor up and back and plotting angle against tension gives a loop rather than a line: at the same tension, the joint sits somewhere different depending on whether you are pulling or releasing. The width of that loop is how wrong a position controller will be.

At zero friction, the loop should close. It doesn't. The result that made this project interesting

With friction set to zero there is still 0.207 rad of hysteresis, which should be impossible. Tracing individual force levels explains it: the finger jumps to a flexed configuration at 2.05 N on the way up, but does not fall back until 1.03 N on the way down. It is bistable.

The cause is under-actuation. One tendon spans both joints — the same arrangement as the long flexors in a human finger — so the two cannot be commanded independently, and the whole mechanism's equilibrium shifts discontinuously when the distal joint reaches its limit.

The distinction matters for control. Friction hysteresis can be reduced with better routing or compensated with a model. Bistability cannot. It is intrinsic to the kinematics, and a controller either plans around it or gets surprised by it.

Holding each level longer shrinks the zero-friction width from 0.207 rad to 0.032 and it is still falling. Chapter Four

Two algorithms, written by hand, on purpose

PPO and SAC from scratch — not because the libraries are bad, but because an answer you can defend has to be one you earned.

Roughly three hundred lines each of PyTorch. Generalised advantage estimation, a clipped surrogate objective, orthogonal initialisation with per-layer gains. Then twin critics, Polyak-averaged targets, a squashed Gaussian actor, and a learned entropy temperature.

Both solve the same task. The interesting number is not either one — it is the distance between them.

That 9.41× gap is an engineering argument, not a benchmark. On a physical robot, every environment step costs wall-clock time and actuator wear, so sample efficiency usually wins. In a simulator running thousands of environments in parallel, samples are nearly free and throughput wins instead. Knowing which regime you are in is the decision.

Plate 19 · Learned policies PPO left, SAC right. Both swing up and hold. Watching the rollout is the cheapest check that a good return was earned honestly rather than by exploiting the simulator.

Chapter Five

The same algorithm, 28× faster, on the same processor

Rewriting PPO in JAX — and what the rewrite reveals about why reinforcement learning is slow.

The PyTorch implementation is a loop: step the environment, append to a buffer, update. The Python interpreter sits in the innermost part of that loop, once per environment step.

JAX inverts the structure. The rollout becomes a compiled scan that never returns to Python, the environment is vectorised across five hundred copies at once, and the whole update is a single compiled function. Nothing interpreted remains in the hot path.

This is why the rewrite is a prerequisite rather than a flourish. In-hand manipulation needs on the order of 10⁸ environment steps. At the PyTorch implementation's rate that is roughly fourteen hours of pure environment stepping before any learning is counted.

Independent confirmation

The port reproduced the original's bug, exactly

The first JAX version ran at 196,000 steps per second and learned nothing at all — flat at −1200, the same symptom the PyTorch version had shown weeks earlier, from the same cause: no reward scaling.

Two independent implementations in two different frameworks failing identically is not a coincidence to be embarrassed about. It is evidence the two agree.

Chapter Six

Muscles are not motors

A whole-body musculoskeletal model with 700 Hill-type actuators over 85 degrees of freedom, and six studies on what changes when the thing pulling the tendon has a state of its own.

Every other humanoid in the standard model library is torque-actuated: one motor per joint, force commanded directly, available torque independent of pose. A muscle is none of those things. It pulls only, its force depends on its own length and shortening velocity, and there are far more of them than there are joints to move.

MS-Human-700 is the closest public model with all three properties. It is not a model of any particular robot, and nothing here claims otherwise. It is a testbed for the control problems muscle-like actuation creates regardless of whether the contractile element is protein or a pneumatic bladder.

The moment-arm matrix has rank 65 of 85, identically at every one of 24 sampled poses. Two numbers follow. The activation null space is 635-dimensional, so activation is not recoverable from joint torque: enormously many patterns produce the same motion and differ only in stiffness. And 20 joint directions are unreachable, six of them the floating base, which no internal force can move.

That last one is the check the rest of the chapter rests on. Across random activation vectors, the generalised force on the three base translation degrees of freedom is 0.0 N, exactly. Newton's third law, not a fit. A non-zero answer would have invalidated everything downstream.

Five patterns explain ninety per cent of the activation and deliver seventy per cent of the force. The gap between the two curves in Plate 23

Activation-space variance is the metric the synergy literature uses, and on this plant it overstates functional adequacy badly. Muscle gains span 25 N to 6,200 N, so a small activation error on a strong muscle is a large torque error. If you are compressing a controller onto a synergy basis, score it on what it delivers, not on what it explains.

Two more results were not the hypothesis. Co-contraction is supposed to stiffen a joint, and here its sign changes with joint angle at every joint measured, negative over roughly 40% of each range. And a command derived from a torque model loses 10.6% of the required joint torque at walking cadence, recovered to 2.0% by inverting the actuator dynamics in closed form.

The model's shipped integration step misintegrates the fastest state it has by 15% of full scale: activation is advanced by explicit Euler at 2 ms against a 5 ms time constant. A 10-90% rise time is not merely inaccurate there, it is unbracketed, because the first step jumps from zero to 0.4 and never lands between the thresholds.

One bug in this chapter is worth naming because it had already produced a published number. The model encodes its knee and shoulder kinematics as 42 equality couplings, and a forward pass computes constraint forces without projecting positions. Writing a driver joint therefore leaves its dependents stale, and every moment arm is then evaluated at a configuration the model does not admit. A torque swing first reported as 26.6× is 1.33× once the dependent joints are projected.

Chapter Seven

A machine that runs on water

Hill muscles are a good model of a muscle and the wrong model of a braided hydraulic actuator. Building the right one, from published specifications, and asking what it takes to fill a leg with them.

The previous chapter found two things a Hill muscle cannot represent for an android driven by fluid: it carries no series elasticity, which is why its co-contraction stiffness changes sign, and its activation lag is a fixed filter rather than a valve moving a volume. So this chapter builds a McKibben-class actuator, a bladder inside an inextensible braided sleeve, and sizes it from figures its manufacturer has published.

The sizing is an inference rather than a fit. A published figure of over 30% unloaded contraction constrains the rest braid angle below 34.4 degrees, on its own, through the closed form for maximum contraction. A published 3 gram fibre at a kilogram of force then sets the radius. Every comparison afterwards is an inequality in the direction the specification states.

Then the arithmetic turns unkind. A braided sleeve's maximum contraction rises as its braid angle falls and has a ceiling, approached and never reached, of 1 − 1/√3 = 42.26%. That is a bound on the actuator class, not on a particular design.

A human leg's muscles need a median excursion of 37.3% of their longest length, and seventeen of fifty exceed the ceiling entirely. They are not a random seventeen. They are the glutei, the adductors, sartorius, gracilis and psoas: the long-throw hip muscles. Anatomy picked exactly the muscles a braid is worst at.

A machine built to match a human's peak capability is 2.7× the machine needed to do a human's actual work. Peak isometric force against the force stance tasks use

Sizing target turns out to matter more than any of it. Summed peak isometric force for one leg is 50,137 N; the force actually used across 277 stance problems is 18,684 N. That is 5.69 litres of working fluid against 2.19, and 5,111 published fibres against 1,905, for the same limb doing the same job.

And the fluid itself is rationed, which is a constraint no torque-actuated humanoid has an analogue of. A motor draws current from a bus that can usually supply it. Here, tracking a stance cycle demands a mean 25.5 litres per minute against a peak of 60.0, so the published 40 litre pump is flow-limited 26.6% of every cycle.

Equal-share water-filling scores 0.139 relative torque error against uniform scaling's 0.417. The torque-weighted policy, which prioritises by how much each actuator moves the joints being tracked, comes second. At the worst instant equal-share fully satisfies 23 of 27 requests and concentrates the entire shortfall on four, while uniform serves none of them fully and puts everybody at 66.7%. Torque error is a norm, so many small correct contributions beat everything being slightly wrong.

Serving actuators in proportion to what they asked for, meanwhile, produces allocations identical to uniform scaling at every timestep. It sounds like a policy. It is uniform scaling, restated.

Chapter Eight

Sensing, switching, and a number that was wrong

Three studies that each set out to measure one thing and found a different one, including one that corrects an earlier chapter of this document.

A musculoskeletal robot is hard to instrument. There is no clean place to put a joint encoder on a tendon-routed skeleton. But close a valve and the fluid is trapped, so any change in contraction has to be absorbed by pressure, and a closed actuator becomes its own encoder. In principle.

The full pressure scale covers between 0.215 and 2.44 mm of travel, under 8% of the stroke, at sub-micron resolution. Superb force and contact sensing; useless for pose. The open-valve alternative, integrating flow, drifts 85% of the stroke in sixty seconds at one per cent flow error, because integration has no restoring term.

Pose is nonetheless fully observable from the pressure vector, rank 5 of 5. The two modes fail in complementary ways, which suggests treating valve closures as position fixes: a limb that occasionally holds still is a limb that stays calibrated.

The actuator fills with a time constant of 0.354 ms. A solenoid running at 100 Hz has a period twenty-eight times that, so each cycle fully charges and fully discharges: the result is a square wave between empty and supply, not a held mid-pressure. Ripple collapses by a factor of 2,565 across the transition, which is a cliff rather than the textbook inverse-frequency rolloff, and fitting a power law across it measures the wrong thing.

Getting above the cliff is the whole game, and a minimum on-time is what stops you: raising the carrier to fix ripple directly worsens duty resolution. A tenth of a millisecond, fast for a solenoid, costs 440×. The cheap fix is to add hydraulic compliance, which directly undoes the lock in Plate 28. Two measurements taken independently, constraining each other.

The finger reproduced Chapter Three to three decimal places, and then broke it. What happens when you vary a parameter nobody varied

Chapter Three reported 0.207 rad of hysteresis at zero friction, argued correctly that friction cannot explain it, and attributed it to snap-through bistability. The first half of that argument still stands. The trouble is the settling cap.

That measurement holds each command until the joint stops moving or a four-second cap expires, and notes that some levels hit the cap. It never varied the cap. Varying it: 0.207 rad at four seconds, 0.117 at ten, 0.055 at thirty, 0.032 at eighty, monotone and still falling. The published figures measure how long the sweep waited.

The qualitative claim survives at every cap tested: hysteresis rises monotonically with tendon friction. That was the study's main result and it is robust. The absolute table, and the attribution of 0.207 rad specifically to bistability, are not. The width trends toward zero, which is what frictionless physics predicts and what incomplete settling looks like.

The braid, for its part, cuts hysteresis by 49 to 85% against an ideal force source, because a compliant actuator cannot hold its command through a snap. Its own absolute numbers are reported as upper bounds: that arm does not settle either, and saying so is the point.

Chapter Nine

What the mechanics choose, and what a rig could measure

Two closing studies. One asks what motion a muscle-driven leg would pick if effort were the only thing that mattered, and gets the shape right and the answer wrong. The other asks what you could learn about fifty actuators from five torque sensors.

Everything up to here took the task as given and asked what the actuators have to do. The first of these turns it around. Metabolic cost per unit distance is U-shaped in step length, and people settle near the bottom of that curve. If a purely mechanical effort model, with no metabolism in it anywhere, reproduced both the shape and the location, that would say the preference is a consequence of the mechanics rather than of physiology.

The shape arrives. There is a genuine interior minimum: halving the optimal step costs 1.85x, doubling it 5.6x, and past 0.94 m the leg cannot produce the required torque at all. Short steps pay a per-step overhead many times over, long ones need large torques at bad postures, and both sides of the textbook argument fall out of mechanics alone.

The location does not. The model picks 0.46 m against a human 0.65 to 0.75, roughly thirty per cent short, and the answer slides two grid cells when the effort exponent is changed from two to five. A prediction that moves with a modelling choice nobody has settled is a weak prediction, and saying so matters more than the central value.

The cheapest way to walk, if effort is all that counts, is not to bend your knees. What the optimiser found, and why the answer is short

The diagnosis is in the postures the optimiser chose. At every step length it drives the knee to its lower bound and holds it there. That is correct, and it is exactly why a quasi-static model is the wrong instrument for this question: a straight leg is cheap to hold and useless at absorbing a landing, clearing a swing foot, or returning elastic energy, and none of those costs exist in a model without dynamics. Bending the knee is what makes long steps affordable, and this model has no reason to do it.

So the honest reading is narrow. Quasi-static mechanical effort explains the shape of the cost curve and not the position of its minimum, and the missing ingredients are the ones the gait literature already names.

The second study is a question you would want answered before building anything. A musculoskeletal limb has fifty actuators and, realistically, five joint torque sensors. Is calibrating it even possible?

It is a rank question, and answerable on paper. Because joint torque is exactly affine in activation, scaling one actuator's peak force scales one column of the plant, so torque is linear in the unknown scale factors. One pose gives five equations for fifty unknowns; stacking poses builds a system whose rank and conditioning are the whole answer.

Sixteen poses make the system solvable. But the condition number is at its worst, around 2,500, at almost exactly the point rank becomes full, and it keeps improving for another three doublings of effort after rank has stopped changing. Rank answers "can you". Conditioning answers "how well". A rig that stopped collecting the moment the problem became solvable would amplify its sensor noise about four times more than one that kept going.

The practical answer is encouraging. At a tenth of a newton-metre of torque noise, which is comfortably inside a good dynamometer, all fifty actuators are recovered to better than 3.2%. At a full newton-metre it degrades to thirty-nine of fifty, and the eleven that drop out share a property: almost no leverage about any joint the rig is watching. They are the psoas group and a small peroneal. That is not a fact about those muscles. It is a specification for where to put the next sensor.

Chapter Ten

Twenty-four bugs, none of which raised an exception

Every result here was checked against something external. This is what that discipline caught.

A learning curve that goes up proves only that a number went up. So each result is validated against a published baseline, an independent implementation, a physical consistency requirement, or data the optimiser never saw. None of the following failures announced themselves — every one produced running code and plausible output.

Missing reward scaling — PyTorch PPO

Returns near −1000 made value loss ~10⁶ against a policy loss of ~10⁻². Global gradient clipping then handed almost the entire gradient to the critic. The policy was starved, not broken.

Missing reward scaling — JAX PPO

The same bug, reproduced independently in a different framework.

Tendon sign convention

Routing sets the sign of the moment arm, gear sets the sign of the force, and the product decides direction. Two attempts each got one half right; the finger crept to the wrong joint limit, compiling cleanly the whole time.

A sinusoid that was not quasi-static

Driving at 0.25 Hz measured dynamic phase lag, not friction — reporting hysteresis that decreased as friction rose. Exactly backwards. Fixed with a settling staircase, which is how the measurement is done on real hardware.

Wrap geoms left in collision detection

Routing pulleys are surfaces, not bodies. Excluding them removed ~18% phantom hysteresis. Found only because a second physics backend refuses cylinder-box collisions and failed loudly where the first was silent.

Truncation bootstrapping under autoreset

In a vectorised rollout the environment resets inside the compiled loop, so the next step's value belongs to a different trajectory. Invisible unless you capture the true next state before the reset overwrites it.

A dataset containing no reality gap

Commanded and measured were the same signal, one step apart. Any fit would have been circular.

A resized geom with a stale bounding radius

Writing geom_size at reset does not update the compiled collision bounds. Pillars sampled taller than their XML size had their table contact culled by the broadphase and settled a centimetre inside the table, contact flickering one tick in five. Found by review before any training run; fixed by writing geom_rbound and geom_aabb alongside the size.

Friction randomisation that was never applied

MuJoCo combines the friction of two geoms by taking the maximum. Against a 0.8 table and 1.0 fingertip pads, most of the sampled [0.6, 1.2] range was inert. The data card would have described a randomisation the physics never saw. Objects now carry contact priority, so the sampled coefficient is the one that acts.

Parked objects remembering the previous episode

Objects a task family does not use are parked out of the workspace — with whatever size the previous family gave them. A 10 cm pillar parked at a 5 cm object's height sat inside the table and bounced for the whole of the next episode. Episode physics depended on what ran before it, and the documented seed-to-episode determinism was false.

A validation set that moved with the data scale

Each grid cell validated on the held-out episodes of its own data prefix, so every point on the hours axis was scored on a different set. Error appeared to rise from sixteen to thirty-two hours. Re-scoring every checkpoint on one common held-out set made every curve monotone.

A step floor that doubled the half-hour cells' epochs

The training budget was "thirty epochs, clamped to at least 8,000 steps". At half an hour of data the floor bound, and those cells trained sixty epochs — twice the recipe — at exactly the scale where the largest model's pretraining appeared to hurt transfer. Rerun at thirty epochs, the effect is not significant. The claim was withdrawn.

Confidence intervals of zero width

Bootstrap percentile intervals on success flags degenerate at 0% and 100%: a policy that fails every episode resamples to itself and reports certainty. Replaced with Wilson intervals, which have correct coverage at the extremes — where a reader most needs to see uncertainty.

A NaN that was telling the truth

A rise time came back NaN. "Fixing" it to fall back on the first sample produced a plausible 25.9 ms that sat neatly beside the converged 31.8 and was entirely an artifact of the step size: at that step the first move clears the 10% threshold, so the window is never bracketed. The NaN was correct output.

Actuator index is not tendon index

Both arrays are length 700 in this model, so colouring tendons by activation index compiles, runs, and renders the wrong muscles. The orderings are a non-trivial permutation. Found because a render lit a strip of shin while the quadriceps it had actually activated stayed blue.

Equality-coupled joints that do not follow their drivers

Forty-two couplings encode the knee and shoulder kinematics, and a forward pass computes constraint forces without projecting positions. Writing a driver joint leaves its dependents stale by up to 0.60, and every moment arm is then evaluated at a configuration the model does not admit. It had already produced a published number: a torque swing of 26.6× that is 1.33× once projected.

A constraint that silently stopped existing

The flow study tracked only the bladder's compliance volume and missed the braid's geometric volume, four orders of magnitude larger. Mean demand read 0.003 L/min, nothing was ever rationed, and all four allocation policies tied at exactly zero error. Every check passed, because a constraint that never binds is trivially respected. Four different algorithms agreeing exactly is the tell.

A bisection bracket that forbade extension

A braid pulled past its rest length pulls harder, which is the state an antagonist pair puts one of its members into. Capping the bracket at rest length pinned the stretched actuator at the bound and gave a flat joint-stiffness curve at exactly k·r², which is the answer you get when the solver has stopped solving.

A pinned solve that returns a number, not an error

One low-pressure point still landed on the bound with an −11 N residual against a nominal 1e−8, and set the reported maximum stiffness for its whole pressure level. The solver now returns a convergence flag and the statistics exclude and count the failures.

A median where a minimum belonged

The achievable-duty check compared the median gap in a discrete set against a closed form and reported a 25% error. The closed form was right: a short grid's top end is irregular and the median picks the leftover up. The resolution floor a controller feels is the smallest gap, not the typical one.

An under-resolved run that looked like physics

At a fixed integration step the fastest carrier got forty samples per period and reported an error that rose with frequency, which reads as a real high-frequency penalty. It was arithmetic. The step now adapts, and the rise disappears.

A different statistic, presented as a comparison

One study measured the maximum separation between two branches while the study it was being compared against measures the mean gap over a trimmed grid. It reported 0.863 rad against 0.207 for the identical case and looked like a finding. Both metrics are defensible; using one of each and subtracting is not.

Infeasible samples averaged away

Cost was the mean over the solvable samples in a step, scaled back up. Long steps then came out cheaper than the optimum, because at the longest reach only six of nine samples could be solved at all and the three impossible ones simply vanished from the average. A step is now feasible only if every sample in it is.

An objective that returned infinity

With unreachable postures scored as infinite, the simplex optimiser's convergence test computes infinity minus infinity, which is not a number, and the search wanders while the library warns. Replaced with a large finite penalty that grows with how badly the torque was missed — after which the optimiser immediately found postures six times cheaper than the profile it had started from.

What reproducibility means here

Every run writes a single append-only log whose first line is a manifest: seed, configuration, git commit, library versions, and a hash of the compiled physics model. Hashing the compiled model rather than the source catches edits that the XML alone would hide, so any figure can be traced to the exact physics that produced it.

Metrics are pure functions of those logs, never computed inside a training loop — so a metric can be redefined and every historical run re-scored without retraining anything. Results report the interquartile mean with bootstrap confidence intervals rather than a mean over three seeds, following Agarwal et al.

Those logs are committed to the repository. They are what the numbers on this page were computed from.
